③ listening · the future of education

A century of wrong predictions

10:00

Press play and listen ONCE without the text. Then decide True or False — click to check. Only after that, open the transcript.

🎧 episode cut · part 1
show transcript (after the answers)

Throughout much of the twentieth century, there have been bold predictions about new technologies that would "revolutionise" education. The invention of TVs was heralded as the start of a new educational revolution: anyone, anywhere, could get access to the best possible teaching, right there on their screen. In the 1960s there was talk of a teaching machine, with which students could learn at their own pace and get personalised feedback. Then there were correspondence courses; then computers and the Internet — many believed this would be the real game-changer, allowing anyone to access a modern-day library of Alexandria. And then the MOOCs, the massive open online courses: you could follow a Harvard course on philosophy, then a computer science course at Oxford. There was virtual reality, trumpeted as yet another tool that would have us all learning in the metaverse. None of these, it is fair to say, have had the revolutionary impact many thought they would. We humans are awful at predicting the future — and the future of education is clearly no exception.

3. MOOCs let you follow courses from Harvard and Oxford.

4. The speaker thinks every new technology has revolutionised education.

③ listening · part 2

The teacher stays — but changes

10:00
🎧 episode cut · part 2
show transcript (after the answers)

The first key theme in today's reports is this: most predictions still have a human teacher at the centre of the learning experience. That is interesting, because many earlier predictions had the teacher disappearing completely. Instead, teachers are expected to move from being "gatekeepers of knowledge" to "choreographers of learning" — organising and guiding the learning experience rather than standing between students and information. And teachers do things technology cannot: they motivate, they inspire, they notice when a student is having a bad day. Artificial intelligence might take over the administrative tasks — the grading, the paperwork — and that would free teachers to spend more of their time actually engaging with students.

The pandemic experiment

10:00
🎧 episode cut · part 3
show transcript (after the answers)

The pandemic forced a global experiment with virtual learning: schools around the world closed, and lessons moved online. The result? Most students and teachers were desperate to get back to the classroom. Virtual learning worked, more or less, but it was a poor substitute for the real thing — the social side of school, the friends, the sports, the conversations before and after class. And while AI and personalised learning pathways certainly hold promise, there is a long track record that suggests technology alone isn't enough.
